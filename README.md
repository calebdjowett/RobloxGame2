Title: Hensen Juang - Journey Back

A Stage Obby style game.

I want the player to start at the very bottom of the ground, a large flat plane. The obstacle course should be a verticle climb style game. 
A massive central pillar structure works best for a vertical climbing Obby, as it gives players a clear sense of progression and keeps the map tightly optimized. You can build one colossal, towering structure in the center of the map -- an endlessly stacked server rack and towering motherboard mixture, and have the obstacle course spiral upward around its perimeter. This allows players at the bottom to look up and see the absolute chaos above them, which builds anticipation, while players at the top can look down to see how far they've come.

To match your theme while keeping development time low, break this vertical climb into distinct visual zones as players ascend.

The ground floor should be the Suburban Kitchen Zone. The entire base of the map is a flat, tiled kitchen floor with a massive oven in the center. Players spawn where he first start nvidia. navigating over giant spatulas and oven mitts to reach the base of the main tower.

The lower-to-mid section becomes the Consumer Graphics Zone. This is where your JTX 1060s and PTX 3060s live. You can reuse the exact same blocky GPU model for these levels, just scaling them up slightly and changing the glowing neon accents from standard green to silver or red as they get higher. The path here should weave in and out of a giant open PC case, forcing players to jump across moving cooling fans and dodge electric arcs between RAM sticks.

The upper section transitions into the Enterprise AI Zone. The environment should shift into a sterile, sleek server room aesthetic floating high in the air. The obstacles here are the H100s and B100s, constructed from imposing black and gold blocks. Players have to navigate rotating server racks, avoid laser beams representing fiber-optic data transfers, and cross the overheating thermal platforms.

The absolute peak is the Stock Market Moon. This is a final floating platform in the sky where the GB200 Superchip monument sits, surrounded by the green zigzag stock lines, "Buy the Dip" signs, the Alisa and Dori Juang statues, and your VIP winner's lounge.

To keep your game running smoothly and your building process fast in the Creator Hub, rely heavily on modular prefabs. Build just one VERY good, detailed and realistic circuit board platform, one very good, realistic, and detailed GPU block, and one fan (very realistic version) as well. From there, just rotate, recolor, and resize those identical assets to build the entire spiral path up the tower.

Aim for 50 to 75 levels. This is long enough to feel like a full journey from the GTX 1060 ground floor up to the GB200 Superchip monument, but short enough that dedicated players can finish it in one sitting.

Each level should take the average player 10 to 30 seconds to complete on their first successful try.

In a Roblox Obby, fast pacing is critical. If a level takes longer than 30 seconds, falling off feels too punishing, and players will leave your game rather than try again.

Here is how you should pace the time across your 50 to 75 levels:

Levels 1–10 (The Hook): 5 to 10 seconds each. These should be practically frictionless. Players are just running and doing basic jumps over stationary GTX 1060s or static server racks. The goal here is rapid progression so the player feels a sense of momentum and commits to staying in the game.

Levels 11–50 (The Core Loop): 15 to 20 seconds each. Introduce moving obstacles here, like the spinning cooling fans or giant swatting spatulas. If a player gets hit and dies, they only lose about 15 seconds of effort, which keeps them motivated to instantly respawn and try again.

Levels 51+ (The Squeeze): 20 to 30+ seconds each. This is where you place your hardest obstacles. Because the levels take slightly longer, dying here is more frustrating. This is exactly the point in the game where players will get impatient and buy your "Skip Stage" microtransaction.

Make sure your custom UI (like the persistent shop button or the "Skip Level" prompt) does not overlap with the mobile thumbstick on the left or the jump button on the right. Ensure that tapping interactive objects (like buying a jacket from a rack) works flawlessly on touchscreens.

Data:
Implement a production-grade `DataService` in strict Luau using `DataStoreService` to persist all player progress (currencies, levels/rebirths, unlocked/equipped items, streaks, settings, and `ProcessReceipt` purchase history). Enforce these anti-data-loss rules:

1. Protected Calls & Retries: Wrap every `DataStoreService` call (`GetDataStore`, `GetAsync`, `UpdateAsync`) in `pcall` with a retry helper (3 attempts with exponential/linear backoff) so temporary Roblox API hiccups never crash a thread or drop a save.
2. Atomic `UpdateAsync` & Session Locking: Never use `SetAsync` for player profiles. Use `UpdateAsync` with a session lock (`{ job = game.JobId, time = os.time() }` with a ~90s timeout for crashed servers) so server-hopping cannot duplicate items or overwrite newer saves.
3. Anti-Wipe Guard on Failed Loads: Never fall back to a blank default profile if a live DataStore load fails or remains locked after retries—kick the player with a friendly rejoin message and block saving for that session so a failed load can never overwrite a player's real save.
4. Schema Versioning & Reconciliation: Include a `reconcile(raw)` function that merges saved tables into the latest default schema so adding new fields in future updates never wipes existing player data.
5. Complete Save Lifecycle: Save automatically every 60 seconds, immediately on critical purchases (`ProcessReceipt` only returning `Enum.ProductPurchaseDecision.PurchaseGranted` after a confirmed save), on `Players.PlayerRemoving` (releasing the session lock), and inside `game:BindToClose` (yielding until all in-flight saves finish).
6. Studio / Unpublished Fallback: Probe `DataStoreService` on startup; if API access is disabled in Studio or the place is unpublished, warn once and run in an in-memory test session mode so playtesting still works out of the box.
